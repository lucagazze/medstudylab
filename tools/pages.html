Drug Accumulation:

Some drugs build up in specific tissues instead of spreading evenly through the body. These reservoirs equilibrate with plasma and release the drug back slowly over time.

It is worth noting that lipid-soluble drugs have a strong affinity for adipose tissue, whereas water-soluble drugs tend to hold higher concentrations in the blood and interstitial space.
🔍 Metabolism ✕
💊

This process, also called biotransformation, allows the liver to convert drugs into active, inactive, or more easily excreted forms.

Half-life (t½):

The time required for the plasma concentration of a drug to fall by half. It determines the dosing interval and how long the drug lingers once it is stopped.

After roughly 4 to 5 half-lives, a drug is considered effectively cleared from the body.

First-Pass Metabolism:

After oral administration the drug passes through the digestive tract, where it is absorbed and carried to the liver via the hepatic portal vein.

In the liver many drugs are metabolised, which reduces the amount of active form reaching the bloodstream.

This initial hepatic step, known as the first-pass effect, sharply lowers the concentration of active drug available for distribution around the body.

Bioavailability (F):

The fraction of an administered dose that actually reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. By definition an intravenous dose has F = 100%, because it bypasses first-pass metabolism entirely.

Pharmacodynamics

The study of the biochemical and physiological effects that drugs produce on the body.

🔑 🔒
A drug binding to its receptor works much like a key fitting a lock. Drugs (the keys) find specific receptors in the body (the locks). That interaction triggers a biological response.
🔍 Types of ligand ✕

AGONIST

A ligand that binds to a receptor and activates a response similar to that of the body's own molecule. Agonists are either full — activating the receptor completely — or partial, activating it only in part.

Specificity:

The ability of a drug to bind selectively to its target receptor rather than to other receptors present in the body. Low specificity means the drug binds several different receptors, which is what produces side effects.

ANTAGONIST

A ligand that binds to the receptor but blocks it, preventing the natural molecule from producing its effect.

Remember: an agonist turns the response on; an antagonist keeps it switched off.

Affinity vs efficacy

Affinity is how tightly a drug binds its receptor. Efficacy is how strong a response it produces once bound. An antagonist has high affinity but zero efficacy.

🔍 Drug selectivity ✕

The selectivity of a drug is its capacity to act specifically on certain target receptors or tissues without disturbing other systems.

Relationship with receptors

Selective drugs bind predominantly to one receptor type, which makes their therapeutic action far more precise.

The greater the selectivity, the more specific the drug–receptor interaction becomes.
Lower selectivity → more unwanted effects

Impact on side effects:

Selectivity is directly linked to how often adverse effects appear. Highly selective drugs tend to cause fewer adverse reactions, because they interact with fewer receptors outside their therapeutic target.

Selectivity is dose-dependent: push the dose high enough and even a selective drug starts hitting its secondary receptors.

Examples and applications:

🫀🩺

Drugs used in cardiac treatment, such as beta-blockers, are a clear example of why selectivity matters: it prevents effects on other systems, like the lungs.

Poorly selective drugs

Drugs with low selectivity can bind to several receptor types at once, producing a broader — and often less predictable — range of effects across the body.

This is why a non-selective beta-blocker can slow the heart and trigger bronchoconstriction, whereas a cardioselective one mostly spares the airways.

Therapeutic window

The range between the minimum effective dose and the maximum dose before toxic effects appear. This interval defines the safety of the drug.

Onset and duration of effect:

The time a drug takes to start working after administration, and how long that effect persists.

⏳

Therapeutic index (TI):

TI = TD50 ÷ ED50

A single number summarising safety. Drugs with a narrow index — warfarin, digoxin, lithium — need plasma monitoring, because the effective and toxic doses sit close together.

The higher the therapeutic index, the wider the margin for error when dosing.

Median Effective Dose (ED50):

💊 ✅

The dose at which 50% of individuals show the desired therapeutic response. It is the standard measure of a drug's potency.

Median Toxic Dose (TD50):

🤒 ⛔

The dose at which 50% of individuals show a toxic response. It is used to assess how safe a drug is.

Median Lethal Dose (LD50):

☠️ 💊

The dose at which 50% of individuals show a lethal response. Together with the TD50 it defines the drug's overall safety margin.

A wide gap between ED50 and TD50 means a large therapeutic index — the drug is easier to dose safely.

Potency vs efficacy

Potency is the dose needed to produce an effect — a lower ED50 means a more potent drug. Efficacy is the maximum effect the drug can achieve, no matter the dose.

A more potent drug is not automatically a better one. Furosemide has a far higher efficacy than bendroflumethiazide — it clears much more fluid at its maximum dose — whatever the milligram potency of either.
